# Competitive landscape

## Define the set by job, not category

Competitors are whatever the buyer would do instead. That set almost always extends past
the category a vendor-comparison page would list.

Always include:

- **The status quo.** Spreadsheets, email, a manual process, tolerating the problem. Usually
  the market-share leader and usually absent from competitive analyses.
- **The internal build.** For technical buyers this is the live alternative, and the one
  most likely to be chosen for the exact segment that feels the pain most acutely.
- **Adjacent tools bought for something else** that cover part of the job as a side effect.
  Bundled-good-enough beats standalone-better more often than founders expect.
- **Services and contractors** solving it as labour rather than product.

A competitive analysis containing only funded startups in the same category has usually
missed the actual competition.

## Per competitor

Capture what predicts behaviour, not what fills a table:

| Field | Why |
|---|---|
| Positioning, in their words | What they claim, which constrains what they can pivot to |
| Who they actually sell to | Often narrower than the positioning implies; read reviews and case studies for the real answer |
| Pricing model and real numbers | Model matters more than number: seat, usage, platform fee |
| The wedge they entered on | Shows the sequence they believe in |
| What they are not doing | Deliberate omissions map the gaps |
| Shipping cadence | Changelog and release frequency, last 12 months |
| Headcount trend | Direction over 18 months beats absolute number |
| Hiring right now | Job postings are the most reliable public roadmap |
| Funding and date | Runway pressure shapes near-term behaviour |
| Recurring complaints | From reviews and issue trackers — where the wedge is |

## Reading health

Absolute size says little about trajectory. Look for direction: shipping cadence slowing,
headcount flat while funding is recent, support responsiveness declining, docs going stale,
job postings concentrated in sales rather than engineering, founder posts shifting from
product to category evangelism.

Do the same in reverse for a competitor that looks small but is compounding.

## Positioning

Choose axes that reflect a real buyer trade-off, and state what high and low mean on each.
Axes that correlate (price vs quality) produce a diagonal line and no information. Axes
tracking a genuine tension — depth vs breadth, speed to value vs configurability, self-serve
vs assisted — separate the field.

Plot the status quo and the internal build alongside the vendors. They occupy positions.

## Gaps

A gap in the landscape is a hypothesis, not an opportunity. For each gap found, ask why it
is empty. Usually one of:

- Nobody wants it — tried and abandoned; look for the corpses
- Structurally unservable — regulation, distribution, unit economics
- Too small to sustain a company, though possibly fine as a feature
- Genuinely unserved, because the enabling change is recent

Only the last is an opportunity, and it needs the enabling change named. This is the section
where analysis most often flatters the thesis, so state which of the four applies and on
what evidence.

## On finding no competitors

Treat as a search failure until proven otherwise. Search the symptom vocabulary rather than
the category name, look for the internal builds, look for abandoned attempts. A genuinely
empty market is rare and is more often a sign that nobody will pay than that nobody noticed.

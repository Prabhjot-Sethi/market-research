# Consumption validation

The question is not whether people have the problem. Almost any plausible problem is real
for someone. The question is whether anyone currently spends money, time or political
capital on it, because that is what predicts whether they will spend on your solution.

## Stated vs revealed

Stated demand is what people say in surveys, interviews and forum posts. It is nearly free
to produce and systematically overstates willingness to pay. Revealed demand is what people
did when it cost them something.

Weight revealed over stated, always. Where you only have stated demand, say so explicitly
rather than presenting it as validation.

## Evidence ladder

Roughly descending in strength:

1. **Existing budget line.** Someone is paying for something to address this now — a
   vendor, a contractor, an internal team. The strongest signal available, because the
   budget already exists and the fight is over who captures it rather than whether it
   is funded at all.
2. **Procurement and tender records.** Public bodies and large firms publish what they are
   buying. Slow-moving, unusually reliable.
3. **Headcount spent on the problem.** Job postings naming the pain, or a team whose
   existence is the workaround. A role costs far more annually than most software.
4. **Custom internal builds.** Engineering time spent building a partial solution is the
   clearest possible statement that the problem exceeds the pain threshold, and simultaneously
   the clearest statement of who your real competitor is.
5. **Adoption of adjacent tools.** Download curves, install counts, review volume on tools
   that partially address it.
6. **Support and forum volume.** Recurring questions, and especially recurring workarounds
   posted by different people.
7. **Stated interest.** Survey responses, upvotes, "we'd love this" comments. Weakest.

## The displacement question

Every purchase displaces something. Name what: a vendor, a contractor, a spreadsheet, an
intern, a team, or nothing at all. If the answer is nothing, the buyer has no existing
budget line and you are asking for new spend, which is a materially harder sale and should
be stated as a finding rather than left implicit.

## Workaround archaeology

The most informative artifact in early validation is the workaround. Find the spreadsheets,
scripts, Zapier chains, contractor arrangements and manual processes people have built.
Each one tells you the problem is worth solving, exactly which part of it hurts, and the
price ceiling — you must be cheaper than the workaround's total cost, including the labour
that maintains it.

A problem with no observable workarounds usually is not a problem anyone is prioritising.

## Disqualifiers

Say so plainly when you find these. They are more useful than supporting evidence:

- The pain is real but the cost of tolerating it is lower than any plausible price
- The person with the pain has no budget and no route to someone who does
- Spend exists but is falling, and the underlying need is being removed rather than served
- The workaround is good enough and free
- The spend is a compliance line item that will disappear when the regulation changes

## Sizing

Build bottom-up: identifiable buyers, multiplied by observable spend per buyer, with both
numbers traceable to evidence IDs. Show the arithmetic.

Published market-size figures are usable only where the methodology is stated and the
source chain reaches a primary document. Most do not. A cited TAM whose derivation cannot
be shown is worse than no number, because it anchors the reader on a fiction.
